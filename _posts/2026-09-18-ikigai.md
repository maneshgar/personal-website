*Ikigai* by Héctor García and Francesc Miralles begins in Okinawa, one of the
world's Blue Zones, where people live remarkably long lives. The scientists who
have studied these places keep arriving at the same few answers: a good diet,
regular movement, strong ties with friends and family, and a purpose in life,
an **ikigai**. Even the smallest habits leave a trace. Okinawans drink tea and
stop eating when they are about 80 percent full, and their blood tests show
fewer of the free radicals responsible for cellular aging.

But the part of the book that stayed with me most is about how we spend our
time. A good life, the authors argue, is built from moments of **flow**: those
stretches when, in Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's words, we are "so involved in an
activity that nothing else seems to matter." Flow asks something of us. We have
to choose activities that absorb us over the easy pleasures that fill our
evenings, and we have to be able to concentrate for a long time, because that is
what any difficult achievement requires. It also needs the right challenge: when
a task is too easy, we get bored and start inventing complexity just to stay
engaged.

To find that kind of work, the book suggests carrying a compass rather than a
map. Borrowing a principle from Joi Ito of the MIT Media Lab, it is more
important to know the direction we are heading than to plan every step. We
should reflect on what we hope to achieve before we begin, then keep that goal
in mind without obsessing over it, because, as Einstein put it, "a happy man is
too satisfied with the present to dwell on the future."

And perhaps the simplest lesson is the hardest to practise: do one thing at a
time. Concentrating on a single task may be the most important factor in
reaching flow. Every time we switch back and forth, we waste time, make more
mistakes and remember less of what we did. Multitasking, the authors note, can
cut our productivity by at least 60 percent.

It's a short book, but a good reminder that a long and happy life comes less
from big decisions than from where we choose to put our attention, every day.
