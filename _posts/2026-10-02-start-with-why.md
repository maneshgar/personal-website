*Start with Why* by Simon Sinek opens with an uncomfortable idea: we make
decisions based on what we *think* we know, and those assumptions aren't always
true. Gathering more data doesn't necessarily save us, especially when a flawed
assumption set the whole process in motion in the first place. One of my
favourite stories in the book makes the point. At an American car plant, workers
hammered every door into place with a rubber mallet to make sure it fit. At a
Japanese plant, there was no such step. "We make sure it fits when we design it,"
they explained, almost sheepishly. They didn't study the problem and accumulate
data to find the best fix; they engineered the outcome they wanted from the very
beginning.

That, in short, is the book's argument. Organizations and people who start with
**why**, their purpose, inspire us, while those who only talk about *what* they
do have to rely on manipulation instead. In the business-to-business world,
Sinek points out, promotions are dressed up as "value adds", but they work the
same way. And when we do choose something because of what it stands for, the
product becomes a symbol: like the products a company makes are proof of its
WHY, a brand can become proof of an individual's WHY too. The lifestyle comes
first.

The difference shows up inside companies as well. Sinek describes a culture in
which people still came to work motivated, but no longer *inspired*, and I liked
the way he separates the two: "Energy motivates but charisma inspires. Energy is
easy to see, easy to measure and easy to copy. Charisma is hard to define, near
impossible to measure and too elusive to copy." It's also why leaders who chase
short-term numbers lose their way, and why Howard Schultz wrote his now-famous
memo warning Starbucks it was losing what made it special. As one CEO put it,
"Wall Street is in the business of making money between now and next Tuesday."

The book reaches beyond business, too. It quotes Martin Luther King Jr.'s
reflection that "any law that uplifts the human personality is just. Any law
that degrades human personality is unjust," a reminder that the most powerful
movements are driven by a clear sense of right and wrong, not by tactics.

The line I'll remember most comes near the end. When you compete against
others, no one wants to help you. "But when you compete against yourself,
everyone wants to help you."
